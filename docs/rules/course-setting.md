# Course setting

Digest of the course-setting principles the engine and the assistant follow, for individual foot orienteering in forest (long and middle distance). Sprint, relays, children's guided courses (RR/TR), ski-O, MTB-O and trail-O are left out.

Sources (see [`docs/sources`](../sources/README.md)):

- **RMK x.y**: Ratamestarikirja (SSL course setter's handbook), chapter x.y. Binding for Finnish events through Lajisäännöt 10.32.
- **IOF App. 2 §x**: IOF Competition Rules 2026, Appendix 2, Principles for course planning. Binding for international events, a guideline for others (§1.2).
- **LS x**: Suunnistuksen lajisäännöt 2026.

Lines marked **Interpretation** are our own reading.

## Aim

- Courses must suit the expected abilities of their competitors; results must reflect technical and physical ability (IOF App. 2 §2.2).
- Golden rules: the character of orienteering as running navigation, fairness, competitor enjoyment, wildlife and environment, media and spectators, safety (IOF App. 2 §2.3).
- Fairness means removing luck from the result as far as possible (RMK 1.3.1). Every competitor must face the same conditions on every part of the course (IOF App. 2 §2.3.2).
- Courses are always compromises with land access, nature, other courses, control load and the event layout (RMK 1.2.6.S).

## Planning order

- Plan as many good route-choice legs of different lengths and character as possible first, then join them with map-reading legs (RMK 1.2.6.S).
- A few very good legs joined by short links are better than many even but weaker legs (IOF App. 2 §3.4.1).
- Planning courses on the map first produces better route-choice legs than collecting interesting control sites in the terrain (RMK 5.1).
- Keeping a good route-choice leg is almost always more important than making its start or end control as demanding as possible (RMK 5.1).
- Connecting legs between terrain parts don't have to be high quality, and it's not worth forcing them (RMK 1.2.6.S).

## Legs

### Types

- **Map-reading leg** (kartanlukuväli): progress close to the course line, following map detail. Most legs are map-reading legs (RMK 1.2.1.SHP).
- **Route-choice leg** (reitinvalintaväli): has at least one route longer than the straight line but faster (RMK 1.2.2.SHP).
- **Leading leg** (johdatteleva rastiväli): no route choice, follows one path or road. The easiest leg type, for beginners (RMK 1.2.5).

### Good legs

- Offer interesting map-reading problems and good terrain, with possible alternative routes (IOF App. 2 §3.4.1).
- Vary leg type, length and difficulty, so competitors use a range of techniques and running speeds (IOF App. 2 §3.4.1).
- Change the general direction between consecutive legs, so competitors must reorient (IOF App. 2 §3.4.1).
- Competitors should concentrate on navigation all the time. Avoid sections with no map reading unless they come from a particularly good route choice (IOF App. 2 §3.7).
- The whole leg should have meaningful content, not just the control (RMK 1.2.1.SHP).
- Courses traditionally start with a map-reading leg, so competitors get to know the mapper's style (RMK 1.2.1.SHP). Competitors should face orienteering problems right from the start (IOF App. 2 §3.3).

### Route choice

- A good route-choice leg has many alternatives, which branch further into sub-alternatives (RMK 1.2.2.SHP, RMK 1.4.3).
- Route-choice legs are usually longish but can be very short. Short ones can surprise competitors who default to "straight is shortest" (RMK 1.2.2.SHP).
- The fastest route may go around or more directly; both are part of orienteering skill (RMK 1.2.2.SHP).
- In hilly terrain, offer route choices where total climb matters (RMK 1.2.2.SHP).
- Small changes to a control's position can add or remove viable alternatives (RMK 1.2.2.SHP).
- Don't over-complicate: competitors may take the "next best" route and save planning time (IOF App. 2 §3.11.5).

### Fairness

- No route choice may give an advantage or disadvantage that can't be foreseen from the map under competition conditions (IOF App. 2 §3.4.2).
- Avoid legs that tempt competitors through forbidden or dangerous areas (IOF App. 2 §3.4.2). Courses should give no benefit from crossing forbidden areas (RMK 1.3.1).
- The fastest route must not involve, for example, swimming across a deep lake (IOF App. 2 §2.3.6).
- Don't route courses through parts of the map that aren't reliable enough (RMK 1.3.1).

### Following and queuing

- Don't load the start of the course with many short demanding legs: followers gain most there (RMK 1.4.3).
- The gain from following is about 6% in forest orienteering (RMK 1.4.3).
- Route choices make competitors think independently and split the field (IOF App. 2 §3.8).

## Controls

### Sites

- Controls are placed at features shown on the map (IOF App. 2 §3.5.1). Controls shall only be placed at points clearly identifiable on the map (ISOM 2017-2 sym. 703).
- The map must be accurate around controls, from every direction of approach (IOF App. 2 §3.5.1).
- Not on small features visible only from close by, unless there are supporting features on the map (IOF App. 2 §3.5.1). A control at the end of a map-reading leg must not be a small or vague feature in an area with nothing else leading to it (RMK 1.2.1.SHP).
- "Bingo" controls (a lone point in an empty area, found by compass and pacing) have long been considered unsuitable (RMK 1.1.3).
- Controls in green or featureless areas have not been valued (RMK 1.2.1.SHP).
- The control feature and its map position must be indisputable. Features that can't be described with IOF control symbols are usually unsuitable (IOF App. 2 §3.5.6).
- Not very close to dangerous precipices (IOF App. 2 §2.3.6). Approaching from the expected direction must not cross a dangerous spot, such as a high cliff (RMK 4.5.S).
- A boulder is a weak map-reading feature where there are many of them (RMK 1.1.1).

### Flag visibility

- The flag should be seen first when reaching the described feature, never hidden (IOF App. 2 §3.5.3). The flag must be visible when the competitor reaches the feature (LS 11.431).
- Don't site controls where flag visibility from different directions can't be judged from the map or description (IOF App. 2 §3.5.1).

### Minimum distances

### Acute angles

- Avoid the "acute angle" effect, where outgoing competitors lead incoming ones to the control (IOF App. 2 §3.5.4).
- Angles sharper than 45° between consecutive course lines have traditionally been considered poor course setting (RMK 1.3.1). Angles below 60° have been viewed with suspicion (RMK 4.5.S).
- Judge the angle by the routes competitors actually run, not only by the course lines. A thicket or hill can hide the outgoing runner; a gentle angle can still expose them in open forest (RMK 1.3.1, RMK 4.5.S).
- **Interpretation:** flag angles below 45° as a warning and below 60° as a note, measured on the computed routes when available.

### Control codes

- Codes are 31–599, excluding 66, 68, 86, 89, 98, 99, 161 and 191 (LS 11.46).
- Avoid similar codes on neighbouring controls, such as 55/56 or 81/181 (RMK 4.5.S).

## Difficulty

- A leg's technical difficulty is roughly the number of decisions to make and features to identify correctly (RMK 1.2.5).
- A control's difficulty depends on the size and visibility of the feature. In increasing order: field corner, boulder near the field corner, banana-shaped knoll near the field corner, depression in that knoll (RMK 1.2.5).
- Beginners' controls are on large collecting features; the next level uses smaller features close to a big attack point; demanding controls usually have no dominant feature nearby (RMK 1.2.5).
- Difficulty can be varied by how closely legs follow line features (IOF App. 2 §3.9).
- The hardest control or leg sets the skill level needed for the whole course (RMK 1.2.5). A good course is more than the sum of its legs: rhythm matters (RMK 1.2.5).
- It's very easy to make courses for novices and children too difficult (IOF App. 2 §3.11.2).

### Class letters

- E (elite), A (normal demanding), B (somewhat easier), C (easy, for occasional orienteers) (RMK 1.3.3, LS 9.2).
- Lower classes have winning times of 50–100% of the main class (LS 11.251), so their courses must also be shorter (RMK 1.3.3).
- "A" means different things in H80A, H12A, H21A and D35A. The letter only relates to the normal level for that class (RMK 1.3.3).

## Physical demands

- Normally fit competitors should be able to run most of the course set for their level (IOF App. 2 §3.11.6).
- Total climb should normally not exceed 4% of the length of the shortest sensible route (IOF App. 2 §3.11.6).
- Physical difficulty should decrease with age in masters classes; take special care with M70+ and W65+ (IOF App. 2 §3.11.6). For H80, avoid dangerous cliff areas, large climbs and very detailed areas that are hard on eyesight; bringing the start or controls higher on a slope reduces climbing (RMK 1.3.3).
- Fast legs with rough navigation can challenge A-level competitors too and shouldn't be avoided by default (RMK 1.2.3.SHP).

## Formats

### Long distance

- Tests map reading, route choice, physical fitness and concentration (RMK 2.S.1).
- Long route-choice legs are at least desirable, and essential in championship events (RMK 2.S.1).
- Aim for varied physical and technical tasks and changes of rhythm (RMK 2.S.1).
- Map scale 1:15 000 is recommended when competitors who take part in international events are expected (RMK 2.S.1, RMK 6.8.1).
- On courses over an hour, drinks every 30 minutes. A drinks control is recommended at national events and compulsory at SM (RMK 2.S.1). Drinks points must not become extra controls on a leg (RMK 4.4.S).

### Middle distance

- 1.5–2 km² of terrain is enough, but it must be detailed, and map accuracy matters more (RMK 2.S.2).
- Some route choice, but route-choice legs can be shorter. Legs are short on average, the shortest a few tens of metres (RMK 2.S.2).
- Changes of rhythm are central: a "compressed, accelerated long distance" (RMK 2.S.2).
- Courses are similar across classes; the shortest age-class courses are about 1.5 km (RMK 2.S.2).
- Must call on detailed map reading and concentration throughout (IOF App. 2 §3.10).

## Start and finish

- Start: a warm-up area, and waiting competitors must not see the route choices of those who have started (IOF App. 2 §3.3).
- The start point (K-point) is at the centre of the start triangle, best on a feature identifiable both on the ground and on the map (RMK 5.2.ST).
- At least the last part of the route to the finish should be a compulsory marked route (IOF App. 2 §3.6).
- Three finish types: continuous marking from the last control, a funnel marking that still requires navigation, or no marking (RMK 4.4.S).
- Continuous marking reveals the last control and weakens its navigational value (RMK 4.4.S).

## Map

### Scale

- 1:15 000 is the ISOM standard and is good for larger long and ultralong events (RMK 6.8.1).
- Otherwise 1:10 000 (RMK 6.8.1).
- 1:7 500 is allowed at the organiser's discretion for classes 14 and younger and 45 and older, except in sprint, and in other classes with the controller's permission (LS 12.13).
- Use the same scale on all maps of a class (RMK 6.8.1).
- Produce maps at the size and scale promised in the invitation (RMK 6.8.1).

### Paper and descriptions

- Avoid oversized map sheets; children may benefit from smaller sheets (RMK 6.8.2.S).
- Always show control descriptions on the map too, even when loose descriptions are given (RMK 6.6).

### Course overprint

- Point feature: circle centred on the feature, whichever side the flag is on (RMK 6.1.S).
- Junction, bend or fork of a line feature: circle centred on it (RMK 6.1.S).
- Contours, knolls, re-entrants and area features: circle centred on the part of the feature where the flag is. A small area feature is centred as a whole (RMK 6.1.S).
- Cut circles and course lines where they hide important features, leaving a lighter gap so the cut is visible (RMK 6.2, RMK 6.3).
- Course lines stop 0.5–1.5 mm before the circle and are always straight (RMK 6.3).

### Out of bounds

- Some areas are out of bounds by their map symbol, such as yards and permanently forbidden areas (RMK 6.5).
- Fields with crops, motorways and railways are not forbidden by the map specification and must be forbidden separately with overprint (RMK 6.5).
- Railways are mostly forbidden to follow or cross at grade (LS 3.61 f, cited in RMK 6.5). Symbol 711 only forbids following, so crossing needs an out-of-bounds area; mark allowed crossings with 710 (RMK 6.5).

## Course search

**Interpretation:** how the search builds a course (D12).

- The course stays inside the mapped area: the area within 100 m of contours, form lines and vegetation or open-land areas, with holes filled. Margins with logos, legends and texts are outside.
- The finish is at the competition centre. The course is a loop around the centre of the reachable terrain, in a direction chosen by the seed. The start is a clear site (control site difficulty at most 2, RMK 5.2.ST) 5–20% of the winning time from the finish and 10–70° ahead of it around the loop.
- Leg times are drawn from a profile per format. Long distance: about one leg in three is a long route-choice leg of 6–12% of the winning time, the rest are links of 1.5–4% (RMK 1.2.6.S, RMK 2.S.1). Middle distance: legs of 2–7% (RMK 2.S.2).
- Each control moves the course round the loop about as far as its share of the time budget. While building, controls are skipped if they break the spacing rule, are closer than 75 m to another control of the course (so the circles, footprint 75 m per ISOM sym. 703, do not overlap), make an angle under 60°, cross an earlier course line, pass within a control circle (37.5 m) of a course line, or need a course line that leaves the mapped area (no marked routes yet). The last control must be within 4% of the winning time of the finish, clear of the finish circle, with an angle of at least 60° towards it.
- Area quality: each site is rated by the demanding control features and contour detail within 150 m, halved in slow vegetation and scaled to the map's best areas. The score rewards the controls' mean quality (middle 40, long 20 points) and the build prefers good areas. Long distance centres its loop on the quality-weighted terrain.
- Middle distance works in a core area of 1.8 km² (RMK 2.S.2: 1.5–2 km² is enough) with the most quality within reach of the finish, plus the final approach. It starts in the core, has no loop rule and is penalised (3 points) for carrying straight on (angle over 150°), so it changes direction often.
- Long distance rewards route choice on its up to four longest legs (RMK 1.2.6.S, IOF App. 2 §3.4.1): 8 points per key leg times a route-choice value that is highest when going straight is 5–30% slower than the fastest route (a real trade-off) and low when straight is fastest or impossible.
- No control sites within 40 m of railways or forbidden areas (IOF App. 2 §2.3.6). Ends of leading features (paths, roads, power lines, ditches) are difficulty 1.
- The course is improved by swapping, removing and inserting controls, reversing a section of it, and moving the last control near the finish. The score is the distance from the target winning time (2 points per percent) plus penalties: 50 per spacing problem, 20 per angle under 45°, 4 per control outside the class's difficulty range, 15 per crossing of course lines, 10 per course line through another control's circle, 5 per percent of winning time that the last leg exceeds 4%, 30 per course line outside the mapped area and 15 per pair of overlapping circles. Angles are checked at every control, including the last one before the finish. An angle under 60° costs 5.
